Halloween is a Catholic word. Seriously.

It is short for All Hallows' Eve. And a hallow is a saint. So Halloween literally means the night before All Saints.

Most people stop at the candy. But the Church has two of the most powerful days of the whole year right after it. November first and November second. And by the end of this video, you will know a way to actually help someone you have lost. This week.

First, you need one idea that ties it all together.

The Catechism says the Church is not only the people you see on Sunday. It exists in three places at once. Some of Christ's disciples are pilgrims on earth. That is us. Others have died and are being purified. And others are already in glory, in heaven.

One family. Three places. And the Catechism says the bond between them is not broken by death.

November first is for the ones in glory. November second is for the ones being purified. Let's go.

November first. All Saints' Day.

So what is a saint? Simple. A saint is someone who is in heaven with God.

Some saints are official. The Church has canonized them. The Catechism says that means the Church solemnly proclaims that they practiced heroic virtue and lived in fidelity to God's grace. And it gives them to us as models and intercessors. People to copy, and people to ask for help.

But here is what most people miss. All Saints' Day is not only for the famous ones. It is for all of them. Every single person in heaven. Including millions whose names we will never know.

The Book of Revelation describes them like this: "a great multitude which no man could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and tongues." That reading is proclaimed at Mass on All Saints' Day.

That crowd might include your great-grandmother. A janitor nobody noticed. A kid who died young. Holy people who were never famous.

This feast is old. In the seven hundreds, Pope Gregory the Third dedicated a chapel in Saint Peter's in Rome to all the saints. About a hundred years later, Pope Gregory the Fourth extended the feast of All Saints to the whole Church.

Now, the big question people ask. Do Catholics worship saints?

No. Worship belongs to God alone. Full stop.

What we do is ask the saints to pray for us. The same way you would ask a friend to pray for you. The only difference is that the saints are closer to God than any friend on earth.

The Catechism says that because the saints are so closely united to Christ, they do not stop interceding for us. When Saint Dominic was dying, he told his brothers: "I shall be more useful to you after my death." And Saint Therese of Lisieux said: "I want to spend my heaven in doing good on earth."

That is not creepy. That is family.

And here is the part that hits different. The Catechism says that all Christians, in any state or walk of life, are called to the fullness of Christian life and to the perfection of charity. That means you. You are not supposed to just admire saints. You are supposed to become one.

Now. November second. All Souls' Day.

This day is for the people who have died in God's friendship, but are not quite ready for heaven yet.

The Catechism explains it like this: "All who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven."

The Church gives this final purification a name. Purgatory.

And let's clear up the biggest myth right now. Purgatory is not a second chance, and it is not a mini hell. The Catechism says it is entirely different from the punishment of the damned. Everyone in purgatory is already saved. They are going to heaven. One hundred percent.

Think of it like this. You are invited to the most beautiful wedding ever. You are definitely going in. But you just walked through a field of mud. You would want to get cleaned up first. Not to earn your invitation. Because you love the one who invited you.

The idea of praying for the dead is not new either. It is right there in the Old Testament. In the Second Book of Maccabees, Judas Maccabeus "made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin."

And the day itself goes back more than a thousand years. Around the year one thousand, Saint Odilo, the abbot of Cluny in France, set November second aside in his monasteries to pray for all the faithful departed. From there it spread across the whole Church.

So how do we help them? The Catechism names it. Above all, by offering the Mass for them. And also by giving to the poor, by doing penance, and through something called indulgences.

And this is where it gets really good. The Catechism says our prayer for them is capable not only of helping them, but also of making their intercession for us effective. You help them. They help you. It goes both ways.

Okay. The part I promised. How you can help someone you have lost, this week.

From November first to November eighth, the Church offers a plenary indulgence for the souls in purgatory. Here is what you do. Visit a cemetery, and pray for the dead. Even a quiet prayer in your heart counts.

And on All Souls' Day itself, you can visit a church and pray an Our Father and the Creed.

A plenary indulgence also has a few conditions. You go to Confession. Yes, everything from our last video. You receive Holy Communion. You pray for the Pope's intentions. And you try to be completely detached from sin, even small sins. If you can't meet every condition, the Church still grants a partial indulgence. So it is never wasted.

This year, November first falls on a Sunday. So you are already going to be at Mass. And November second is the very next day.

Here is a prayer you can use at the cemetery. It is short. Memorize it today. "Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them. May they rest in peace. Amen."

So here is your challenge. Write down the names of people you have lost. A grandparent. A friend. Anyone. Then this week, go to a cemetery, say that prayer, and say their names.

Because death does not end the family. It just means some of us are in different rooms.
